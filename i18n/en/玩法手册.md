# Amazon Web Services User Group Cloud Tycoon · Player Manual

> This manual explains how to get started playing. It takes about ten minutes to read. For the details and numbers behind each rule, see the Host Rulebook (主持人规则书.pdf). In the web version, click “How to Play” at any time to open this manual.

> **Disclaimer**: Certain generative AI services from Amazon Web Services featured in this game are available only in Amazon Web Services Regions outside mainland China. The Amazon Web Services User Group presents them only to help you learn about cutting-edge technology and grow your business overseas. Cloud services in the Amazon Web Services China Regions are operated by Sinnet and NWCD; refer to the China Regions website for details. In-game prices are fictional and do not represent actual pricing; $ is the in-game currency.

## 1. The game in one paragraph

You run a company that is starting up on Amazon Web Services. Move around the board and **buy Amazon Web Services services**. When other players land on your services, they **pay you rent**. **Upgrade your services, add capacity, and complete architectures** to collect more. At the same time, you must **survive production incidents** and **keep your bills under control**. The last player who has not gone bankrupt wins, or the first player to reach all six Well-Architected pillars wins (the higher the difficulty, the more each pillar requires). The gameplay is inspired by classic Monopoly-style board games.

1. Open the game web page, pick the number of players (2–4), the difficulty and the win conditions, and click “Start game”. Player 1 goes first. Players who go later start with a little more cash (players 2, 3 and 4 get +$50, +$100 and +$150), which makes up for the cheap squares that earlier players get to pick first.
2. Each player is randomly dealt a role (if “Choose from 3 roles” is ticked on the start screen, each player picks one of 3 cards). Roles differ in starting cash, ability and signature skill. There are 12 in total (with Reseller Mode on there are 3 more Reseller roles; see Section 10):

   **Signature skill**: a comeback move. When your net worth (cash + services + upgrades) falls below half of your starting cash (for a $1600 start, below $800), click “Signature skill” on your own turn to use it. You can use it once per game. When you use it, the role’s own skill resolves first, then a “Strategic Acquisition” follows: you buy one service (with its upgrades and capacity) from the opponent with the highest net worth in the game, at the sell-back price, and the money goes to that opponent. If you don’t have enough cash, you go into debt and the debt rules apply. The amounts in the skills are base amounts: afterwards they rise with traffic by up to half (at most 2.5×), ×1.2 more in the Full Game, and do not rise in Hell Mode (Unicorn’s “New Funding Round” and Master Reseller’s “Annual Rebate” follow their formulas and do not rise). The button tooltip and the player card in the web version show the current multiplier.
3. **Pick a mode**: for fun, pick **Party Mode** (only common services, mystery box squares everywhere, lots of card drawing, 10 rounds in about 15 minutes). To count the bills seriously, pick **Beginner / Standard / Full Game**. To learn and prepare for certifications, pick **Study Mode** (required questions, mistake log, no round limit). You can also pick a **card multiplier**: 1×, 1.5×, 2×, or random each round.
4. The start screen defaults to Standard. For your first game, switch to **Beginner** and keep the default win conditions (Classic + 15-round limit, about 20 minutes per game). Try Standard and Full Game once you are comfortable. To learn Amazon Web Services along the way, turn on the **Quiz While Playing** switch under step 02 “Difficulty” on the start screen (it turns on automatically if you pick Study Mode).
5. Not enough players? Tick “CPU” after a name and the computer plays that seat, so you can start even alone.
6. To play on everyone’s own computer or phone, go to step 04 on the start screen: the host clicks “Create room”, sets a password, and sends everyone the 6-digit room number and the password. The others open the same `index.html` and click “Join room”. Each player acts on their own device on their turn, and the screen syncs in real time. If someone disconnects, they rejoin using their in-game name and return to their old seat. If the host’s device is refreshed, the host creates the room again and clicks “Resume last game”, and everyone joins with the new room number and their in-game names.

## 3. How a turn works

1. **Roll the dice** and move forward by the number rolled.
2. **See where you landed**:
   - A service nobody owns → you can Buy On-Demand (full price, safe). EC2, ECS, EKS and SageMaker AI can also be bought on Spot (you name your own bid, at least 20% of the original price; others can’t see your bid, and if another player’s guess is high enough they can take it from you). Discounts from answering a question when buying and discounts from roles and cards all multiply together (stacked discounts).
   - Another player’s service → pay rent.
   - Your own service → you can **add a feature** to it, **add a unit of capacity**, or use **FIS to draw a fault card and attack an opponent** ($40–$100, you pay whatever the drawn card costs; choose one of the three).
   - Health Event / re:Invent → draw a card and follow it.
   - Bill squares and corner squares → do what the square says.
3. **Do something else** (optional): manage assets, trade with others, or quiz an opponent (only one of these per turn). Pay $100 to quiz one player (once per game; the question comes from the color group of the square they are standing on; if they answer correctly they keep the $100, and if they answer wrongly they pay you $200). Or quiz the whole table (once every 3 laps, counted by how many times you have passed Startup Credits; at least two opponents, one question each, $50 each; a correct answer keeps the $50, a wrong answer pays you $100). You can also pay to make an opponent draw a Health card (once per lap; the price is 1.5× the cost of drawing a re:Invent card; you can pick a target, choose at random, or pay double to make everyone draw one). In the Full Game you can also sign Savings Plans / RI and take Actions.
4. When everything is resolved, click “**Next player**” to end your turn (CPU players end automatically). If you roll doubles you roll again, but three doubles in a row in the same turn means “you got throttled” and you go straight to the Maintenance Window.

## 4. How to make money

### 1. Collect rent

The more expensive the service, the more upgrades, and the more capacity, the higher the rent. Services billed by usage (Serverless, plus S3, Route 53, CloudFront, KMS, WAF and Bedrock, which charge by request or token) also have rent that varies with the dice number the other player rolls: a bigger number means more traffic and more rent. When the owner completes a whole color group (several squares of the same color), even services with no upgrades collect double rent. In Standard, Full Game and Study Mode, the first 2 rounds are a **new-account protection** period: each rent is capped at $50 (the squares also show $50), and from round 3 the full price applies.

### 2. Complete architectures and collect combined rent

Put together several services that really work together and you complete an architecture. **When another player lands on any one service in the architecture, they pay you the rent of every service in the whole architecture added together.** This is the most profitable play in the game. If a service is part of several active architectures at once, those architectures **charge in a chain**, and a shared service is counted only once. There are 48 architectures in total; where it says “A or B”, having either one is enough. For architectures that are active on the board, the rent on the square has an orange background and shows the total for the whole set; hover over it to see how much each service contributes.

An architecture has 3 to 7 services (3 to 5 required, the rest optional). For “A or B”, having one is enough, and if you have both, both are collected. A service marked “optional” is not needed to complete the architecture, but if you have it, it is collected too. In the architecture diagrams, → means flowing downstream, ⇄ means asking something and coming back (verifying a token, calling a model), + means working together, and ← means data flowing in from behind.

Some architectures also give extra benefits. For example, “High-Availability Entry” makes the EC2 upgrade to “Cross-AZ Auto Scaling” (the Multi-AZ level) half price, and “DDoS Protection” removes the fixed fee for WAF and makes the first two upgrade levels half price.

**Wildcard services** (5): CloudWatch, CloudTrail and Config (monitoring group) can attach to any architecture. KMS can only attach to an architecture that has storage or a database (S3, EBS, EFS, RDS, Aurora, DynamoDB, ElastiCache, Redshift, OpenSearch). VPC can attach as long as the architecture has at least one service that can connect to a VPC: any service in the compute, containers, database, storage, analytics or AI groups, or ELB or API Gateway (Lambda can connect to a VPC, API Gateway can do private APIs, and S3 and DynamoDB use VPC endpoints); optional services and “pick one of” services also count. An architecture made up only of services from the security, monitoring, Route 53, CloudFront and application integration groups (SQS, EventBridge, Step Functions) cannot attach a VPC. Each wildcard service can attach once to one of your architectures: it fills in a missing service, and if the architecture is already complete it merges in and its rent is collected together. An architecture can take only one wildcard service, and a wildcard service that is already in the architecture cannot attach to it again. Once attached it can’t be changed (the re:Invent card “Had a partner review the architecture” lets you rebind it once for free). If you sell it, trade it to someone else, or it is taken offline by DDoS, any architecture that relied on it falls apart. CloudWatch attached to an architecture that has your own online Lambda or EC2 means “Woken up by an alarm at 3 a.m.” is not a penalty. Drawing the re:Invent card “Solutions architect’s advice” lets you fly straight to the service your architecture is missing.

## 5. How to spend money: different services have different bills

In an ordinary Monopoly game you buy a property and only collect rent. Here, **after you buy a service, it keeps costing you money according to its own billing model.**

| Service type | Examples | Each lap, when passing Startup Credits | When you collect rent |
|---|---|---|---|
| IaaS / Containers | EC2, EBS, ECS, EKS | Pay running cost (even when idle) | All yours |
| PaaS | RDS, Aurora, Redshift, SageMaker AI | Pay running cost, higher than IaaS | All yours |
| Serverless | Lambda, DynamoDB, SQS | Pay nothing | Pay 15% request fees |
| Billed by storage / monthly fee per item | S3, EFS, ECR, KMS, Route 53, WAF | Fixed monthly fee of $5 (not charged for KMS if you use only Amazon Web Services managed keys) | Pay 15% request fees |
| Billed per token | Bedrock | Pay nothing (pay if you add a model unit) | Pay 25% token cost |
| Subscription | Shield Advanced | Fixed $20 | All yours |

So:

- Serverless has no burden early on, and costs nothing when nobody visits, which suits the early game.
- Instance-type services burn money even when idle, but when players land on them all the income is yours, so they suit squares that everyone walks past.
- To save money, buy on Spot (only EC2, ECS, EKS and SageMaker AI can use it; in real life only these have Spot capacity): you name your own bid, at least 20% of the original price (Game Studio: 10%; Hell Mode excepted). The lower your bid, the more you save. Only you know your bid: other players who land on it can only guess, and a guess that is 10% of the original price above your bid takes it from you (you get half of your bid back as compensation). A guess that is too low just pays you the rent. Later you can pay the difference plus 10% of the original price to convert it to On-Demand, and at full price nobody can take it. Financial Compliance Company must bid at least 40% of the original price both when buying on Spot and when grabbing Spot.
- In the Full Game you can sign **Savings Plans** with Amazon Web Services or buy **Reserved Instances (RI)**, where **1 lap counts as 1 year**:
  - Savings Plans, 1 year: no upfront payment; you commit to spending a fixed amount each lap, and running costs for EC2, ECS, EKS, Lambda, SageMaker AI and for RDS, Aurora, ElastiCache and OpenSearch get 25% off.
  - Savings Plans, 3 years: covers only EC2, ECS, EKS, Lambda and SageMaker AI, at 45% off; databases (including OpenSearch) stay at full price, because in real life Database Savings Plans exist only as 1-year terms.
  - RI: pick one EC2, RDS, Aurora, ElastiCache, Redshift, OpenSearch or DMS, pay everything upfront, save 35% for 1 year or 55% for 3 years, and its running cost is waived for the term.
  - If you sell a service back to Amazon Web Services, you still pay the commitment. ELB, EBS and Kinesis can’t use them, same as in real life.
- When short of cash, EC2, RDS, Aurora, Redshift and ECS can be **taken offline**, like a mortgage in Monopoly: you get half of the purchase price back as working cash, no rent is collected while offline, and running cost drops to a $5 storage fee per lap (ECS has none). It stays offline for at least 3 rounds, after which you decide when to bring it back online (pay back that half plus 10% of the original price); nothing is deducted automatically. While it is offline, another player who lands on it can pay the original price to take it away. Services that can’t be stopped in real life, like OpenSearch and ElastiCache, can’t be taken offline. Party Mode has no taking offline.
- EC2 costs an extra $5 per lap for a public IPv4 address (a real charge since 2024); it is waived if your VPC has a private subnet.
- A service you no longer want at all can be **sold back to Amazon Web Services**: you get back **80%** of what you actually spent on purchase price, upgrades and capacity added together, so more upgrades means more money back. You no longer pay its running cost, and the square becomes available for anyone to buy again. Removing only the upgrades or capacity and keeping the service refunds half.
- **Not buying means not buying**: if the player who lands doesn’t buy, the square stays with Amazon Web Services and other players can’t bid for it. When landing on EC2, ECS, EKS or SageMaker AI you can buy it on Spot with your own bid. If a service is already on Spot, a player who lands on it can guess a price to grab it: a guess that is high enough takes it and they don’t pay rent this time; a guess that is too low pays rent as usual.
- On your own turn, before or after rolling the dice, you can pay 4% of your net worth (minimum $50) to **draw a re:Invent card**, once per turn (not allowed while you are in the Maintenance Window).

## 6. How to get stronger: upgrades and capacity

When you land on **your own service**, you can add a feature or add capacity (details below), or use FIS to attack an opponent: draw a random fault card and pay whatever the drawn card costs (Inject API throttling errors $40, Disconnect the network to a dependent service $40, Stop an EC2 instance $60, Terminate an instance $60, Simulate an AZ power outage $100). The opponent takes the hit on the spot. Choose one of the three:

### Add a feature (up to 3 levels)

- **Instance-type services** (EC2, RDS, EKS and so on) are **single-AZ** when you buy them, and you need to upgrade to the level that includes **Multi-AZ**: for RDS, Aurora and EKS this is Level 1 “Multi-AZ”, and for EC2 it is Level 2 “Cross-AZ Auto Scaling” (Level 1 is automatic AMI). EBS volumes are always single-AZ; ECS services spread tasks across AZs by default.
- Other services span multiple AZs by nature, and all three levels are features. For example:
  - ECR: basic scanning → enhanced scanning → cross-Region replication
  - Lambda: higher concurrency quota → provisioned concurrency → container image + Web Adapter
  - Bedrock: Nova Lite → Nova Pro → cross-Region inference (the higher the tier, the more each token costs and the higher the rent; cross-Region inference counts only as elastic scaling in one place, not as multi-Region disaster recovery)
  - VPC: private subnet + NAT (you pay extra for the NAT) → Session Manager → Transit Gateway
- Many features come with built-in protection: Multi-AZ survives an AZ power outage, Auto Scaling survives a traffic spike, enhanced scanning survives a CVE, backups survive accidental deletion… The service details list them all.

### Add capacity (up to 4 units)

Add nodes or concurrency to a service: more instances for EC2, more nodes for EKS, more concurrency for Lambda, more shards for Kinesis, more instances for SageMaker AI, more model units for Bedrock… Each unit makes rent **+25%**. For instance-type services, each extra unit also raises running cost a little per lap; adding concurrency to Serverless does not raise running cost.

> You can only upgrade when you land on your own service. Once you have completed a whole color group, you don’t have to wait to land: you can add capacity at any time in “Assets”.

## 7. How not to get burned: Health Events and protection

Most Health Event cards are bad news, but almost every one has a matching protection. Build your architecture in advance and you can dodge them:

| What you face | How to defend |
|---|---|
| An AZ power outage / single point of failure | Upgrade instance-type services to Multi-AZ; managed and global services mostly already span AZs; if you complete “High-Availability Entry”, EC2’s “Cross-AZ Auto Scaling” upgrade is half price (attaching only an ELB does not count as Multi-AZ) |
| A service goes down in a Region | Upgrade to the multi-Region level |
| A wide us-east-1 outage | Upgrade each service to its multi-Region level (those without it become Impaired); global services like Route 53 and CloudFront are unaffected |
| EC2 instance hardware retirement | Upgrade EC2 to “Cross-AZ Auto Scaling” to replace the instance automatically; or upgrade ELB to Level 3 “Load balancing + Auto Scaling” and EventBridge to Level 2 “Health Event notifications” |
| Traffic spike | Upgrade instance-type services to the “survive traffic spikes” level (EC2 Auto Scaling, ECS service auto scaling, EKS Karpenter auto scaling, RDS read replicas and the like; EBS, SageMaker AI, DMS and OpenSearch don’t have this level), or complete the “Order Peak Shaving” architecture; other services are unaffected. If all your services survive, collect $100 |
| Service quota exhausted | Lambda higher concurrency quota, or a CloudWatch alarm to catch it early |
| DDoS | Shield Advanced, WAF, CloudFront (Shield Standard is on by default for free) |
| SSH brute force | Hold the “Automated Threat Response” or “Security Group Auto-Tightening” architecture; upgrade VPC to Session Manager, or pay $50 to borrow someone else’s; ECS with Fargate and EKS with Auto Mode are also safe; with only GuardDuty you still catch it in time, and you pay $50 instead and the instance does not become Impaired |
| High-severity CVE | Hold the “Vulnerability Management” architecture; Inspector scanning for EC2; ECR enhanced scanning or Inspector image scanning to protect containers; runtimes of managed databases and Serverless are patched by Amazon Web Services |
| Accidental data deletion | Backup-type features (S3 versioning, DynamoDB point-in-time recovery, EBS automatic snapshots, EC2 automatic AMI, EFS automatic backup) |
| An S3 bucket set to public | A KMS customer managed key, CloudFront origin access control, or upgrade Config to “Auto-remediation” (automatically sets the bucket back to private); with only GuardDuty you still catch it in time, and everyone pays half compensation instead |
| Root key pushed to GitHub (Health card) | The “Automated Threat Response” architecture (GuardDuty detects it and automatically revokes the key); the corner square “Root Key Leak” has no protection, and landing on it sends you to the Maintenance Window |
| Certificate expiry | Upgrade ELB or CloudFront to HTTPS (ACM renews automatically) |
| Woken up by an alarm at 3 a.m. | Attach the CloudWatch wildcard service to an architecture that has your own online Lambda or EC2 |
| Failed the security audit | Hold the “Security Baseline” architecture, or 2 or more security group services, or one compliance upgrade (Config managed rules, CloudTrail log file integrity validation or organization trail, KMS automatic rotation, WAF managed rules, GuardDuty enabled in all Regions, Inspector Lambda scanning, Cognito MFA, API Gateway authentication, AgentCore Identity); Financial Compliance Company is already immune |
| Someone changed the production configuration by hand | Upgrade Config to “Auto-remediation” so configuration drift is changed back automatically |

A service hit by an event becomes **Impaired** and collects no rent until the start of its owner’s next turn.

If you really can’t dodge it, you can use an Enterprise Support card, a postmortem marker or a drill marker to cancel it, or pay a management fee to a player who is immune to that card and ask them to take it on for you (at least $20, and they must agree). Any card that makes you “skip a round” can be changed to paying money and handling it immediately, then moving as normal. If you are penalized and nothing cancels it, from the Standard game on you take 1 **tech debt**: each one adds $20 to running cost per lap; at 2, each upgrade costs $50 more; at 3 you need a “Big Refactor”: skip a round and pay $300, or don’t skip and pay $400 (tech debt collected when drawing a Health card scales with the card multiplier). Each refactor clears 1: it costs $50 with 1 on hand and $100 with 2, plus $30 more from round 10 on, so the longer you wait the more it costs.

---

**Same-color jump**: services of the same color are spread across the four sides of the board. When you land on your own service (upgraded or not), you may fly to the next service of the same color ahead of you (like Ludo), one jump only. If the square you jump to is unowned you can’t buy it; if it belongs to someone else you pay rent.

**Not just dice**: cards such as satellite link, DataSync, cross-Region expansion and the Global Accelerator fast lane fly you straight to a place. Flying forward and same-color jumps still collect money when you pass Startup Credits or the User Group Meetup. Cards such as DNS misconfiguration, release rollback and traffic hijack send you back or swap positions with another player, and these do not count as passing. When moving backward takes you around the start (for example, drawing DNS misconfiguration from a Health square), it counts as going back a lap: the next time you pass Startup Credits moving forward you collect no money and pay no running cost.

## 8. Corner squares

## 9. How to win

- **Classic mode**: when everyone else is bankrupt, you win.
- **Rent goes up as the game goes on**: all rent is × 1.75 from round 5, × 2.5 from round 10, × 3.25 from round 15 and so on. As the business grows, traffic keeps increasing, and late in the game a single good square can bankrupt someone.
- **Quarterly earnings and end of support for old versions** (Standard and up): every 3 laps there are quarterly earnings, and the player with the lowest net worth collects $150 in funding, while the player with the highest pays a $100 compliance review fee into the Jackpot. Each time the re:Invent deck is reshuffled, every player rolls one die, and the player with the lowest number returns one versioned service that has no upgrades and no added capacity (RDS, Aurora, ElastiCache, OpenSearch, EKS, Lambda, Glue, DMS, Bedrock; pick the cheapest one) and gets half of its purchase price back.
- **Bankruptcy**: when you can’t pay, you can first remove upgrades and capacity in Assets, take services that can be stopped offline, or sell services back to Amazon Web Services at 80% of what you actually paid to raise cash; you can also declare bankruptcy directly. If selling everything still doesn’t cover the debt, you go bankrupt automatically, and your services are split among creditors in proportion to what you owe each (the share owed to Amazon Web Services goes back to Amazon Web Services). On your own turn you can also click “Declare bankruptcy” to concede voluntarily; your assets go back to Amazon Web Services and you can’t name someone to give them to.
- You can pick several win conditions, and whoever reaches one first wins.
- **Well-Architected mode**: the first player to satisfy all six pillars at once (Operational Excellence, Security, Reliability, Performance Efficiency, Cost Optimization, Sustainability) wins. The web version shows each player’s pillar progress in real time. Each pillar is scored by “how many you have”: for example, each CloudWatch and CloudTrail you hold counts as 1 point of Operational Excellence, each security service counts as 1 point of Security, and each service that naturally spans AZs, such as S3, DynamoDB and Lambda, counts as 1 point of Reliability (instances such as EC2 and RDS count only after you upgrade them to Multi-AZ yourself). Beginner needs just 1 point per pillar; Standard needs 1 point per pillar, 12 points across the six; Full Game needs 2 points per pillar; Hell Mode needs 2 points per pillar, 18 points across the six. On the web page, hover over a pillar (tap on a phone) to see your score and what is still missing.
- **Hell Mode**: Full Game + all gameplay DLCs, Rapid Deploy and the question bank, with required questions, and after you roll the dice you may also get a random question drop (correct +$50, wrong −$50). Cards ×2, rent +1.5× every 5 rounds, a correct answer reduces rent by only 30%; you collect only $150 at the start square, and selling back to Amazon Web Services returns only 60%; there is no new-account protection, and every role gets one extra Hell restriction. Choose it only if you want a challenge.
- **Round limit** (on by default, 15 rounds, about 20 minutes per game): when the rounds run out, players are ranked by net worth, which ensures a game always finishes. If you leave it unticked, a game still ends after at most 50 rounds (Study Mode has no round limit).
- **Settle now**: if time is up or you don’t want to keep playing, click “Settle now” in the “Game” menu and the game ends with a ranking by valuation.
- **Time limit mode**: when time is up, players are ranked by valuation. If you click “Exit to start screen” in the “Game” menu midway, the timer pauses, and when you come back and click “Resume last game” it keeps counting.

## 11. Tips for new players

1. In the first few laps, buy services with low running costs: Serverless services like Lambda, SQS and DynamoDB cost nothing when unused (once Lambda is upgraded to Level 2 provisioned concurrency it costs $10 per lap). S3 costs only $60 to buy and EFS $100, and each costs just a fixed $5 monthly fee per lap.
2. Focus on one architecture and build it: combined rent is far stronger than a single service.
3. If you buy an instance-type service, upgrade it to Multi-AZ early: the AZ power outage and single point of failure cards are made to hurt single-AZ instances.
4. VPC matters: a player without VPC has to pay the VPC owner to upgrade an instance to Multi-AZ; upgrading VPC to Session Manager also protects against SSH brute force, and others can pay to borrow it.
5. Don’t spend all your cash at once: running costs are due every lap, and bill squares and event cards also take money. If your cash is too low to pay even once, you may be forced to sell off assets.
6. Watch out for Bedrock: it is billed per token, so the bigger the number the other player rolls, the more you collect, but you pay 25% token cost. It has no running cost; unupgraded, its single rent is about the same as a service at a similar price, and after upgrades it grows more slowly. It is also needed to complete three architectures that can’t be built without it (GenAI Knowledge Base (RAG), Managed Agent and AI Agent Workflow), as well as to complete the AI group.

## 13. FAQ

**Q: Which services don’t need me to set up Multi-AZ myself?**
Look at the color of the type text at the bottom of the square. Green text (Serverless and most managed services, such as S3, Lambda, DynamoDB, SQS, and also ECS) has cross-AZ high availability built in, and AZ power outage cards can’t hit them. Orange text (instance-type services such as EC2, RDS, Aurora, EKS) are single-AZ when bought, and you need to upgrade to the level that includes Multi-AZ (mostly Level 1; for EC2 it is Level 2, “Cross-AZ Auto Scaling”). Red text (EBS) is always single-AZ. Blue text (Route 53, CloudFront, Shield Advanced) means global services. To survive a whole Region having problems, every type needs the multi-Region upgrade.

**Q: What’s the difference between selling back to Amazon Web Services and taking a service offline?**
Taking offline is a temporary stop that gets you half back as working cash. The service is still yours, bringing it back online costs that half plus 10%, and meanwhile other players can pay the original price to take it away. Selling back to Amazon Web Services means giving it up completely: you get 80% of what you actually paid for the purchase price, upgrades and capacity, the service returns to Amazon Web Services, and whoever lands on it first can buy it, including you. So if you are short of cash but still want to keep it, take it offline; if you are sure you don’t want it, sell it.

**Q: Why doesn’t Route 53 have “Multi-AZ”?**
Route 53 is a global service and is not in any single AZ. Its upgrades are features such as health checks and failover routing.

**Q: How do CloudWatch, CloudTrail and Config charge?**
These three squares count as one group billed by usage: rent = the number the other player rolled × a multiplier. The multiplier is highest when you have all three, and it goes up further after upgrades.

**Q: Do architecture combined rent and single-service rent stack?**
No. When a player lands on a service in an architecture, you collect the total for the whole architecture once, and the single-service rent is not added on top. If a service is in several active architectures at once, those architectures charge in a chain: the rents of all services involved are added up, and a shared service is counted only once. For example, if you have ELB, EC2, RDS and Route 53 and another player lands on EC2, “Classic Three-Tier Web” and “High-Availability Entry” are both active and the rents of all four services are added together.

**Q: What are the held cards for?**
After you draw one, you keep it in front of you and use it when needed. Enterprise Support lets you leave the Maintenance Window for free or cancel one Health Event card. Spot capacity drops your next minimum Spot bid to 10%. Cost Explorer waives one bill square or cancels “Month-end bill blew up”. MAP gives 30% off your next purchase of an IaaS, PaaS or containers service. Workshop makes your next wrong answer count as correct (landing and answering, being quizzed by an opponent, and spot checks all count). Root cause is upstream lets you pass your next Health Event card to another player to take (if they are immune to that card, it is voided). In the web version, hover over the card name on the player panel (tap the card name on a phone) to see the description.

**Q: How much should I bid on Spot?**
The lower your bid, the more you save, but the easier it is to be taken. Others can’t see your bid and can only guess: a guess that is 10% of the original price above your bid takes it, and you get half of your bid back; if they guess too low, they just pay you rent. For a service you want to keep long term and upgrade, either buy On-Demand directly, or pay the difference later (plus 10% of the original price) to convert it to On-Demand.

**Q: Where does the Jackpot come from and where does it go?**
Both bill squares, Health Event fines and the $50 from the Maintenance Window go into the Jackpot. Landing on User Group Meetup and choosing “raffle” gets you at least $100 and at most the whole Jackpot; drawing “Got Free Tier credits” also takes all of it. Passing User Group Meetup (without landing on it) collects $50.

**Q: Can the web version save my game?**
Yes. The browser saves automatically, so after you close it and open it again, just click “Resume last game”. One browser stores only one game: if an unfinished game exists, starting a new one asks you first. Don’t play in two tabs at the same time; whichever tab continues last is the one that counts.
